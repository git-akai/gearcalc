Measuring the form factor off the profile

Lewis form factors are tabulated because, historically, nobody could compute the real root geometry. This tool generates the exact trochoid — undercut included — and validates it against a simulation of the cutter that would make it. So the form factor is measured off the profile rather than looked up, which is why undercut, profile shift and tooth thickness modification need no special cases: they change the profile, and the profile is what was measured.

Everything drawn below comes from the same code the product runs. The question this sheet answers is whether the construction is doing what it should — which is far easier to judge by looking than by reading an assertion.

Correction: the 30° tangent is a convention, not the model

You spotted that the tangent lines do not meet the end of the moment arm, and that the mismatch grows on undercut teeth. That is real, and it is more than a drawing problem — it is the signature of an approximation failing.

The cantilever model's original form is the Lewis inscribed parabola: a cantilever whose outline is a parabola with its vertex at the load carries uniform bending stress along its length, so the largest such parabola inscribed in the tooth — drawn dashed violet, vertex at the end of the moment arm — touches where the real tooth is weakest. The 30° tangent is a later simplification that ISO and AGMA adopted because it is easier: it is independent of where the load acts, which is precisely the property the cantilever model is not supposed to have.

One correction to the expectation. Under the parabola the tangent lines still do not pass through the vertex — they cross the centreline at exactly 2y_v − y_t, above it. What sits at the end of the moment arm is the parabola's vertex, not the tangents. So the mismatch you saw is diagnostic rather than directly correctable: the right reading is that the 30° construction ignores the load point, and the fix is to change the construction, not the drawing.

Both are now implemented and both converge to their own closed-form rack limit — 2.0632 for the 30° tangent, 2.1588 for the parabola. They are different constructions, not an approximation and its exact form, so the gap does not close. The parabola is consistently the more conservative, by the amount printed in each caption above: +13.7% on the undercut z=12, +2.9% on a well-formed z=17 with positive shift.

A finding that came out of implementing it

On a rack — and on any large tooth — the largest inscribed parabola touches the straight flank, not the fillet, 0.54 module above where the fillet ends. A fillet-only search finds no solution at all, which is how the first implementation failed at z=300. The search now covers both curves and reports which one it touched; small and undercut teeth touch the fillet, large ones the flank.

Correction: the tangent lines were drawn mirrored

In the first version of this sheet the 30° tangents leaned the wrong way — outward, toward the tip, instead of inward. The tangency points and every number were correct; only the drawn line was wrong, because the diagram rebuilt the line from the 30° angle alone and an angle does not fix which way a line leans. Climbing the fillet toward the tip the tooth narrows, so the tangent must lean in.

The diagram now draws the tangent vector the solver actually computed, and a test asserts its sign as well as its angle. Reconstructing a quantity the code already knows was the mistake; the fix is to carry it.

The check that matters

Comparing a form factor against a remembered table proves only that two sources agree, and a misremembered table is worse than none. So the reference here is the rack limit: as tooth count grows the gear tooth approaches the one a straight rack cuts, whose critical section is closed-form — straight flanks at α to the centreline, a circular fillet tangent to the flank and the root line, load at the tip corner.

That reference shares no code with the implementation. It never mentions an involute, a trochoid, or the module under test. It is derived from the rack's own geometry in about fifteen lines.

Reading the diagrams

Two things are worth checking by eye. First, the red chord should sit where the tooth is visibly narrowest in bending terms — low in the fillet, not at the root circle and not up on the flank. Second, on the undercut gears (z=9, and z=12 at x=−0.30) the fillet visibly eats into the flank, the chord narrows, and the form factor rises sharply — 4.17 and 4.88 against 2.96 for an unshifted z=17. A tabulated factor cannot follow that, because the tables are indexed by tooth count and shift alone.
